Board brief · five minutes · September 10, 2026

Show Your Work

The site has not changed in two months. What changed is underneath it: a workspace where a person and several AI agents work at once, and every step leaves a receipt a student could be graded on.

01 · What it is

A workspace, not a chatbot

Think of a school computer lab. There is one shared machine that holds the real work, and a row of desks. Each agent gets its own desk: an isolated copy of the project with its own tools. When a desk's work is ready, it is checked and moved onto the shared machine. That move is called a landing, and every landing writes a receipt. Anything that landed can be undone. The person in charge reads one page.

The shared machineThe real project. Nothing gets here without a receipt.

Desk 2Agent ports the painter

Desks are created and torn down in minutes. Two people on two machines used the same shared machine today without stepping on each other.

02 · Why it is educational

Every step is "show your work," made literal

A program run in this workspace leaves a record of each step: what it read, what it wrote, how long it took, and a fingerprint of the code that did it. That record can be replayed on another machine, byte for byte. The student's claim about their own program becomes something a grader can check rather than take on trust.

  • Replay proves. Same inputs and same code fingerprints give the same result on the grader's machine. Identical fingerprints on two students' steps are the same work, whoever typed it.
  • Undo is free. Every landing can be reverted with one command, and the revert leaves its own receipt. Students can be brave.
  • A rubric with no teacher in it. Before an agent's change may land, a cold reader who has seen only the hand-off page must explain, in plain words, what changed and why. If the explanation does not match the real change, it is refused. Tonight one change was refused this way, its hand-off was fixed, and it passed on the second read.
  • The question habit. Anyone unsure writes a one-line question at the root of the project instead of guessing. That habit is most of learning to program. There are 91 such questions on record today.

05 · The caveat to say first

Red pencil

Identical fingerprints prove the same computation, not the right answer. A grader still needs a reference solution. The workspace makes work visible and checkable. It does not decide what is correct.

06 · The decision in front of the board

One experiment, one term

Whether the homework-sized piece above is worth running with real students for one term. What it would need: a small group, a teacher willing to be the reference, and a rule that a student's records stay on their own machine unless they choose to share them. What it would answer: does a record that shows every step change how students explain their own programs.
